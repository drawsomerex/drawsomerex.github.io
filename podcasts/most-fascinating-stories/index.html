Episode 4: Caesar and the Pirates Who Underpriced Him

In 75 BC, twenty-five-year-old Julius Caesar was captured in the Aegean Sea by Cilician pirates. Insulted by their modest twenty-talent ransom demand, Caesar demanded they more than double it to fifty talents, treated his captors as his personal servants, and promised to return and crucify every one of them—a promise he ruthlessly kept.

The pirates expected the young aristocrat to break down in tears and plead for mercy. Instead, Julius Caesar burst into arrogant, dismissive laughter. Caesar looked the pirate chieftain up and down with supreme disdain and told him that he was an ignorant peasant who clearly had no idea what kind of man he held in his hands. Caesar declared that demanding twenty talents for a man of his illustrious lineage and future greatness was a personal insult. He ordered the pirates to tear up their demand and told them that he would not permit himself to be ransomed for a penny less than fifty talents. The pirates stared in utter bewilderment. Fifty talents amounted to three thousand pounds of pure silver—a king's fortune. Believing their captive was either a madman or the wealthiest prince in Rome, the pirates agreed. Whenever Caesar felt like taking a nap, he sent one of his servants to command the pirate captains to lower their voices, forbidding them from singing, shouting, or drinking near his tent while he rested. The pirates, amused by his bizarre arrogance and eager to protect their fifty-talent prize, quietly obeyed. Caesar spent his days in rigorous literary pursuits. He exercised on the sandy beach, challenged the pirate champions to running races, and composed elaborate Latin poems, dramatic speeches, and rhetorical essays. In the evenings, he would assemble the pirate crew around the campfire and read his compositions aloud in a ringing, theatrical voice. When the illiterate pirates listened with glazed, uncomprehending eyes and failed to applaud with sufficient enthusiasm, Caesar berated them furiously to their faces, calling them uncultured barbarians and illiterate simpletons. Honoring their maritime code, they rowed Caesar and his servants out to a neutral sandbar and set them ashore on the mainland near Miletus. Half the pirates were slaughtered where they slept beside the campfires. [George - Storyteller] ACT VI: THE CROSSES OF PERGAMUM Caesar put his former captors in heavy iron chains and sailed directly north to the provincial capital of Pergamum, locking the pirates in the deep stone vaults of the city prison. Junius, however, saw an opportunity for immense personal enrichment. Caesar did not argue with the corrupt governor. He nodded politely, walked out of the praetorium, and rode straight back to the prison at Pergamum. Exercising his own personal authority, Caesar ordered the prison guards to drag the pirates out to the execution grounds outside the city walls. Remembering that the pirates had treated him reasonably well during his captivity, Caesar demonstrated what Roman stoicism considered genuine mercy: Before having his soldiers nail the men to the wooden crosses, he ordered the executioners to slit their throats with a razor, sparing them days of agonizing exposure under the blazing Mediterranean sun. Julius Caesar had kept his word to the exact letter. In the dusty hills of Pergamum, thirty crosses stood against the sky—a brutal, unforgettable demonstration that when Caesar made a promise, the world would bleed to see it fulfilled. Episode 3: Nixon's Midnight Vigil at the Lincoln Memorial

In May 1970, five days after the Kent State shootings and with Washington D.C. besieged by 100,000 anti-war demonstrators, President Richard Nixon slipped out of the White House unannounced at 4:15 A.M. to speak face-to-face with student protesters at the Lincoln Memorial.

Episode 2: Eisenhower and the Hellish 1919 Mud Convoy

In the summer of 1919, Lieutenant Colonel Dwight D. Eisenhower volunteered for the U.S. Army's first transcontinental motor convoy. Across 3,251 miles of dirt, collapsed bridges, and desert quicksand, the grueling two-month ordeal inspired the modern Interstate Highway System.

Rebecca - Host

Welcome to Most Fascinating Stories, where we uncover the real, well-sourced, and lesser-known tales behind history's most prominent figures. I'm your host, Rebecca. Today, we turn our gaze to Dwight David Eisenhower. History remembers him as the Supreme Allied Commander who orchestrated the liberation of Europe on D-Day, and as the two-term President who transformed the economic landscape of America by signing the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956. Today, millions of drivers travel seamlessly across the United States on fifty thousand miles of asphalt and concrete, rarely asking where the vision for this colossal highway system came from. The truth is, it was born not in a boardroom or a congressional committee, but in the blinding dust, broken axles, and knee-deep mud of an agonizing, forgotten military expedition in 1919. Here is George with the story. [George - Storyteller] ACT I: THE RESTLESS YOUNG OFFICER In June of 1919, twenty-eight-year-old Lieutenant Colonel Dwight D. Eisenhower was in a foul mood. The First World War had just ended in Europe, and while his West Point classmates like George Patton had earned medals and glory in the trenches of France, young Eisenhower had spent the war stuck in Pennsylvania, commanding a tank training center at Camp Colt. He was an exceptional administrator and a master tactician, but he felt his military career was effectively over. Peacetime was shrinking the army, promotions were frozen, and Eisenhower was desperate for a challenge. That challenge arrived in the form of a bold, unprecedented announcement from the War Department: The United States Army was going to attempt the first transcontinental motorized convoy in American history. They planned to drive a massive military column from Washington, D.C. all the way to San Francisco, California. The purpose was twofold: to test the durability of modern military vehicles under peacetime field conditions, and to thank the American public for their wartime sacrifices through a series of parades across the nation's heartland. Eisenhower immediately volunteered to go as an official observer for the Tank Corps. Along with his close friend Major Sereno Brett, Eisenhower packed his field gear, polished his leather boots, and prepared for what was billed as a triumphant cross-country parade. He had no idea that he was stepping into a sixty-two-day survival trial. [George - Storyteller] ACT II: DEPARTURE FROM THE ZERO MILESTONE On the sunny morning of July seventh, 1919, the convoy assembled at the Zero Milestone on the Ellipse, just south of the White House. The scene was magnificent. Brass bands played patriotic marches; Secretary of War Newton Baker delivered a stirring speech; crowds cheered and waved American flags. Lined up along the street were seventy-nine heavy military vehicles, manned by nearly three hundred soldiers and two dozen officers. There were massive five-ton Class B Liberty trucks, ambulances, light delivery vans, mobile repair trailers, field kitchens, motorcycles with sidecars, and even a colossal six-ton pontoon bridging truck. At precisely eleven-fifteen A.M., engines roared to life, exhaust filled the air, and the great convoy rolled west. For the first few days through Maryland and Pennsylvania, the expedition seemed like a glorious holiday. The Lincoln Highway was paved with asphalt and macadam. The trucks cruised comfortably at twelve to fifteen miles per hour, and in every town, thousands of citizens greeted the soldiers with platters of fried chicken, homemade apple pies, and iced tea. Eisenhower enjoyed the camaraderie, smoking his pipe in the passenger seat of an open staff car. But as soon as the convoy crossed the Ohio River, the paved roads abruptly disappeared. Ahead lay two thousand miles of raw, unpaved American dirt. [George - Storyteller] ACT III: THE QUAGMIRES OF THE MIDWEST In 1919, outside of major cities, American roads were little more than packed-earth wagon trails mapped out in the nineteenth century. And when the convoy hit the Midwest, summer thunderstorms descended with unyielding fury. In Illinois, Iowa, and Nebraska, torrential rains turned the black prairie soil into a bottomless quagmire of thick, gumbo clay. It was not merely mud; it was an adhesive, sucking sludge that clung to wheels like wet cement. Ten-ton cargo trucks sank to their axles within minutes. Their solid rubber tires spun uselessly, digging trenches four feet deep. The convoy ground to a complete standstill. Parade uniforms were quickly abandoned. Eisenhower stripped off his officer's tunic, rolled up his sleeves, and waded into the muck beside the enlisted men. County road bridges throughout the rural Midwest had been built for horse-drawn wagons carrying a ton of hay, not for heavy steel military trucks weighing eight to ten tons. Time after time, as a Liberty truck rolled onto a wooden trestle bridge, the beams would groan, crack, and collapse into the creek below. The convoy was forced to halt while military engineers cut down nearby trees with two-man saws, rebuilding bridges on the spot just to advance fifty feet. Progress slowed from fifty miles a day to less than fifteen. Men slept in wet blankets by the side of the road, exhausted and covered in grime. [George - Storyteller] ACT IV: THE DESERT OF SALT AND QUICKSAND If the Midwest was a trial by mud, the American West was a trial by fire. By the time the battered convoy reached Wyoming and Utah, the temperature soared over one hundred degrees. The dirt roads of the plains were replaced by blinding alkali dust that penetrated every gasket, carburetor, and soldier's lungs. Radiators boiled over every ten miles. Local guides warned them that the southern trail across the salt flats was treacherous, but there was no alternative route. Beneath a thin, brittle crust of dried white salt lay an ocean of soupy, grey quicksand. The heavy trucks broke through the salt crust almost immediately. Within an hour, twelve heavy vehicles were swallowed up to their chassis in burning saline mud. Tires could find no purchase whatsoever. The heat was suffocating. To survive, the soldiers were forced to perform an agonizing labor: they unloaded heavy wooden planks from the supply trucks, carried them forward on foot, laid them beneath the tires, drove the trucks forward twelve feet, picked up the planks from behind, and carried them forward again. For four grueling days, in a blinding expanse of white glare, Eisenhower and his men moved like ants across the desert, advancing at an excruciating pace of less than three miles per hour. Mechanical carnage was everywhere. In his logbook, Eisenhower meticulously recorded the damage: broken fan belts, burned-out clutches, cracked steering knuckles, stripped differentials, and snapped drive shafts. Yet, amidst the exhaustion, Eisenhower marveled at the resilience of the men. Mechanics worked through the night by lantern light, fashioning replacement parts from scrap metal, determined that not a single vehicle would be abandoned. [George - Storyteller] ACT V: THE GOLDEN GATE AND THE 37-YEAR VISION Finally, on September sixth, 1919—sixty-two days after departing the White House—a battered, dented, dust-caked column of trucks rolled down the slopes of the Sierra Nevada, boarded a ferry in Oakland, and arrived in San Francisco. They had covered three thousand two hundred and fifty-one miles in sixty-two days, averaging a miserable five point six miles per hour. They had endured ninety-six major breakdowns, twenty-nine serious vehicle accidents, and had destroyed countless wooden bridges. The official parade in San Francisco was celebrated with medals and banquets, but Eisenhower was deeply troubled. In his formal report to the War Department, the young Lieutenant Colonel issued a stark and prophetic warning: The road network of the United States was utterly primitive and completely incapable of supporting military movement. If a foreign adversary launched an amphibious invasion of either the Atlantic or Pacific coast, the United States Army could not even transport troops and supplies across its own continent to defend the nation. The lesson burned into Eisenhower's soul. A quarter of a century later, General Eisenhower stood in Europe as Supreme Commander, watching Allied armies race across Germany on the modern, divided Reichsautobahn. He immediately connected the dots back to the summer of 1919 in the Utah mud. In 1953, when Eisenhower was inaugurated as the thirty-fourth President of the United States, fixing America's broken roads became one of his highest personal priorities. On June twenty-ninth, 1956, President Eisenhower signed the Federal-Aid Highway Act, launching a twenty-five-billion-dollar initiative to construct forty-one thousand miles of high-speed, controlled-access interstate highways—the largest public works project in human history. Today, that system bears his name: the Dwight D. Eisenhower National System of Interstate and Defense Highways. Whenever we drive at seventy miles an hour across the American heartland, we are traveling on a vision forged in the sweat, grit, and mud of a young soldier who spent two months pushing broken trucks out of the desert sand. [Rebecca - Host] CONCLUSION Eisenhower's 1919 convoy is a powerful testament to how personal hardship can shape national destiny. Without those sixty-two miserable days in the Midwest mud, the Interstate Highway System that connected modern America might never have been built. [George - Storyteller] ACT I: THE CURSED ESCORT In the second week of November nineteen forty-three, a thick, cold fog blanketed the waters off Hampton Roads, Virginia. Sliding quietly out into the Atlantic under the cloak of night was the mightiest warship in the United States Navy: the newly commissioned battleship USS Iowa. She was an eighty-eight-thousand-horsepower fortress of steel, carrying nine sixteen-inch guns and armor plates over a foot thick. Aboard was a passenger whose presence was the most closely guarded secret of the war: President Franklin D. Roosevelt. Accompanied by Army Chief of Staff George C. Marshall, Admiral Ernest King, and his closest diplomatic advisers, Roosevelt was embarking on Operation Sextant, crossing the Atlantic to meet Winston Churchill in Cairo and Joseph Stalin in Tehran. It was to be the first face-to-face summit of the Big Three leaders, a meeting that would decide the timing of D-Day and the postwar partition of Europe. For security against German U-boat wolfpacks that infested the Atlantic sea lanes, the Iowa was surrounded by a protective screen of cruisers and destroyers. Among these escorts was a fresh Fletcher-class destroyer: hull number five-seven-nine, the USS William D. Porter, commanded by Lieutenant Commander Wilfred Walter. To her crew of mostly green recruits, the Porter was their ticket to glory. But from the moment she pulled out of Norfolk, the ship seemed cursed with astonishing misfortune. While maneuvering away from the dock, the Porter's stern anchor had hooked onto the railing of a neighboring sister destroyer, ripping away stanchions, railings, and a lifeboat before freeing herself. Captain Walter had delivered a stern dressing-down to his young crew, hoping that humiliating mishap would be their last. It was only the prelude. [George - Storyteller] ACT II: DEPTH CHARGES IN THE STORM Twenty-four hours into the voyage, the convoy ran into ferocious North Atlantic seas. The wind whipped up towering whitecaps, and the destroyers rolled violently in the heavy ocean swells. Suddenly, a massive underwater explosion shook the entire ocean. A thunderous shockwave hammered against the hull of the Iowa. Aboard the battleship, sirens wailed into the storm: General Quarters! Gun crews sprinted to their battle stations; lookout binoculars swept the gray horizons; the Iowa's massive turbines surged to life, heeling the ship into violent zigzag maneuvers. German U-boats were presumed to be attacking the President's flagship. Depth-charge crews stood ready on all destroyers, scanning the foaming waves for periscopes. But there were no German submarines in sight. Minutes later, a shamefaced signal flashed across the water from the William D. Porter. An improperly secured depth charge had rolled off her stern rack during a heavy wave roll and detonated upon reaching its pressure depth. Admiral Ernest King was apoplectic. To broadcast an accidental depth-charge explosion in the middle of the Atlantic was like lighting a flare for every Nazi submarine within a hundred miles. Captain Walter was reprimanded via blinker light, and the men of the Porter hung their heads in disgrace. They desperately needed an opportunity to prove their competence. [George - Storyteller] ACT III: BALLOONS AND BLUE SKIES The opportunity seemed to arrive on Sunday, November fourteenth. The convoy was cruising east of Bermuda, and the weather had cleared into brilliant sunshine and calm blue seas. President Roosevelt, wearing his trademark fedora and clutching a long cigarette holder between his teeth, was brought onto the Iowa's teakwood deck in his wheelchair. He loved the sea; as a former Assistant Secretary of the Navy, Roosevelt was in his element. To entertain the Commander-in-Chief, the Iowa's captain arranged an anti-aircraft gunnery demonstration. Crewmen released large silver weather balloons high into the cloudless sky. Moments later, the Iowa's five-inch, forty-millimeter, and twenty-millimeter guns opened up in a roaring symphony of fire, shredding the silver balloons into ribbons. FDR smiled broadly, puffing his cigarette and applauding the gunnery. Across six thousand yards of open water, Captain Walter of the William D. Porter watched the demonstration. Here was his chance to redeem his ship in the eyes of the President and the high command. Walter ordered his crew to conduct a simulated torpedo drill, using the distant, colossal silhouette of the Iowa as their mock target. [George - Storyteller] ACT IV: FIRE TWO: THE RUNAWAY TORPEDO On the Porter's deck, the torpedo mount was manned by Torpedoman Third Class Lawton Dawson. In a live-fire combat situation, the Mark fifteen torpedoes carried eighteen hundred pounds of high explosives. For a dry drill, Navy regulations strictly mandated that the small explosive primer charges used to expel the torpedoes from their tubes must be removed. Dawson removed the primers from tubes one and three. But on tube number two, in the excitement and noise of the exercise, he made a catastrophic oversight: he left the primer in place. On the bridge, Captain Walter shouted the standard drill command: Mount two, stand by! Fire two! Instead of a hollow metallic click, a terrifying whoosh echoed across the deck. An exhaust plume of white compressed gas billowed into the air. Before the horrified eyes of the crew, a two-ton, fully armed, live torpedo splashed into the Atlantic, its propeller screaming to life at forty-five knots, churning a deadly white frothy wake heading straight for the Iowa. Utter chaos engulfed the bridge of the William D. Porter. Captain Walter stared in disbelief, turning pale as chalk. A live torpedo was speeding directly toward the President of the United States. [George - Storyteller] ACT V: WHEEL ME TO THE RAILING They had less than two minutes before impact. Strict radio silence was in effect to conceal the mission from Axis listening stations. Desperate to warn the Iowa without breaking radio protocols, Walter ordered his signalman to flash an urgent warning with the Aldis lamp. The signalman, shaking with adrenaline, aimed the lamp at the Iowa's towering bridge and began frantically clacking the shutter. Torpedo in water, steer right! he intended to say. Instead, in his blinding panic, the signalman transposed letters. What the Iowa's lookouts read was: Porter is backing up full speed! Aboard the Iowa, the bridge crew blinked in irritation. They flashed back: Porter, clarify message. Seeing that visual signals were failing, Captain Walter threw naval regulations out the window. He grabbed the TBS radio microphone, broke radio silence, and screamed over the fleet's open frequency: Lion, lion, come right! Hard right! Lion, look right! Torpedo in the water! Inside his stateroom, President Roosevelt felt the violent lurch of the deck. Secret Service agent Mike Reilly rushed into the room, shouting that a torpedo was inbound. Most leaders would have ducked for cover inside the conning tower. But Roosevelt's eyes lit up with intense curiosity. Wheel me to the railing, Mike, Roosevelt insisted. I want to see it! Reilly wheeled the President out onto the deck to the starboard railing. With his fedora firmly on his head, Franklin Roosevelt watched the foaming silver track of the torpedo race across the Atlantic directly toward his ship. At the very last moment, the Iowa's sharp evasive turn paid off. The torpedo sliced through the water, missing the stern by roughly twelve hundred yards, detonating in the churning wake. A magnificent geyser of white foam and saltwater erupted hundreds of feet into the sky. The concussion shook the deck of the Iowa, spraying fine ocean mist onto Roosevelt's face. The President let out a low chuckle, removed his cigarette holder, and turned to his aides with a grin. [George - Storyteller] ACT VI: UNDER ARREST IN BERMUDA AND THE PARDON Aboard the Iowa's bridge, however, nobody was smiling. Admiral King and Captain McCrea were utterly convinced they had just survived an assassination plot. Suspicion immediately fell upon the William D. Porter. The Iowa swiveled all nine of her massive sixteen-inch turrets—guns capable of firing twenty-seven-hundred-pound armor-piercing shells twenty miles—and locked them squarely onto the diminutive destroyer. The Porter was ordered to stand down, surrender its logs, and report directly to naval authorities in Bermuda under armed escort. Upon arriving in Bermuda, Marines boarded the William D. Porter and placed the entire crew—officers, sailors, and cooks alike—under military arrest. It was the only time in United States naval history that an entire ship's company was arrested en masse. Interrogations were fierce. Naval intelligence combed through every crewman's background, searching for German saboteurs. Finally, Lawton Dawson, weeping in remorse, confessed that he had inadvertently left the primer in tube number two, and in his panic, had thrown the spent primer casing overboard to hide his mistake. Dawson was court-martialed and sentenced to fourteen years of hard labor. But when the verdict reached the Oval Office, President Roosevelt intervened personally. Writing to the Secretary of the Navy, FDR stated that no harm had been done, that it was clearly an accident born of training frenzy, and asked that Dawson be pardoned and restored to duty. The Navy complied, but they never quite forgave the Porter. The destroyer was exiled to the frozen, fog-choked outpost of the Aleutian Islands. For the remainder of the war, whenever the William D. Porter pulled into a naval anchorage, other American ships would hail her over the radio with the same sardonic joke: Don't shoot, boys, we're Republicans! Roosevelt's unflappable calm at the railing, asking to be wheeled closer to watch the weapon approach, captures the indomitable spirit of a leader who refused to let fear dictate his life.
